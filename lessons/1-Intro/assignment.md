Checkers: From Ancient Board Game to AI Milestone
The Past: Origins and Early AI
Checkers, also known as draughts, is one of the oldest games in human history, with roots tracing back thousands of years. Its simple rules — diagonal movement, mandatory captures, and king promotion — make it easy to learn, yet its strategic depth has captivated players for centuries.
Checkers holds a unique place in AI history because it was the subject of one of the very first machine learning programs ever created. In 1952, Arthur Samuel at IBM began building a checkers-playing program on the IBM 701, one of the company's first commercial computers. Wikipedia What made Samuel's work revolutionary was not just that the computer could play checkers, but that it could learn to play better over time. Samuel introduced mechanisms by which his program could learn from games it had already played — it recorded each position and whether that position eventually led to a win or a loss, and by incorporating these values into subsequent decisions, the program improved the more it played. IBM Samuel called this process "machine learning," a term he coined that remains central to artificial intelligence today. IBM
Samuel's program used what we now recognize as temporal-difference learning and alpha-beta pruning — techniques that became foundational in AI research. He also had the program play thousands of games against itself as a way of learning. Wikipedia In 1962, after extensive self-play training, the program defeated a self-described checkers master named Robert Nealey. IBM This was a sensational demonstration that captured public imagination and even caused IBM's stock price to jump.
The Present: Chinook and the "Solved" Game
The next major chapter came with Chinook, a checkers program developed starting in 1989 by Jonathan Schaeffer and his team at the University of Alberta. Chinook's algorithm included an opening book from grandmaster games, a deep search algorithm, a move evaluation function, and an end-game database for all positions with eight or fewer pieces. Wikipedia Notably, unlike Samuel's approach, all of Chinook's knowledge was programmed by its creators rather than learned with artificial intelligence. Wikipedia
Schaeffer started Chinook with the initial goal of winning the human world checkers championship. ScienceDaily The program lost in the championship match in 1992 but won it in 1994, becoming the first computer program to win a human world championship in any game ScienceDaily — a feat recognized by the Guinness Book of World Records.
But Schaeffer was not finished. He set out to fully solve the game. After 18-and-a-half years and sifting through 500 billion billion checkers positions, Schaeffer and his colleagues built a program that cannot be beaten. ScienceDaily In 2007, they published their proof in the journal Science: perfect play by both sides leads to a draw. Wikipedia Checkers became the most complex game ever mathematically solved.
The AI Future: Deep Learning and Beyond
Although checkers itself is now "solved," it continues to serve as a valuable testbed for modern AI techniques. Researchers today use checkers to experiment with deep reinforcement learning — the same family of methods behind breakthroughs like AlphaGo. Modern projects demonstrate how to use Deep Q-Learning to create AI agents capable of playing checkers at a reasonable level, training through self-play with reward signals rather than hand-coded heuristics. Paperspace
The broader significance is clear. The techniques pioneered through checkers — self-play, temporal-difference learning, reinforcement learning — now power systems far beyond board games. Reinforcement learning has been applied successfully to robot control, autonomous driving systems, and even training large language models through reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF). Wikipedia Samuel's simple idea of a program that learns from its own experience has evolved into one of the most powerful paradigms in modern AI.
Conclusion
Checkers may be a simple game, but its impact on AI has been profound. It gave us the term "machine learning," produced the first self-learning program, became the first game where a computer won a world championship against humans, and was the most complex game to be fully solved. From Samuel's IBM 701 in 1952 to deep reinforcement learning agents today, checkers has served as a mirror reflecting the evolution of artificial intelligence itself — from hand-crafted rules to self-learning systems, from narrow computation to the foundations of general intelligence.
